# LLMs & the Attention Economy — Week 1

## Series theme

The psychology of what AI tools are doing to the way people think, decide, and work. You're not teaching — you're noticing things and saying them out loud. You happen to be living this as an engineer who got laid off and is trying to build a business, which gives you a firsthand lens, but the subject is the psychology, not the app.

**Tone:** Observational, honest, slightly unsettled. You're thinking through something, not presenting conclusions.

**Audience:** Anyone who works with AI tools daily and has a nagging feeling that something shifted in how they think or work but hasn't been able to name it.

**Platforms:** LinkedIn + X (post natively to both, don't cross-link). Vertical (9:16).

## Video 1: "A Different Speed of Feedback"

**Post:** Monday or Tuesday
**Length:** ~25 seconds
**Format:** Talking head. Look at camera. No screen share, no overlay text, no cuts.

### Script — personal version

> "I could've written this as a text post in 30 seconds. But that's kind of the problem I want to talk about."
>
> "I'm an engineer — 12 years — got laid off, and instead of going back to the 9-to-5 I'm trying to build something on my own."
>
> "And I noticed that I can build features with AI all day. But the stuff that doesn't have that feedback loop — talking to users, sending emails, sitting with uncertainty — started feeling almost impossible."
>
> "I don't think it's laziness. I think my brain got recalibrated to a different speed of feedback. And I don't think I'm the only one."

### Script — less personal version

> "I could've written this as a text post in 30 seconds. But that's kind of the problem I want to talk about."
>
> "AI tools make certain kinds of work feel incredibly fast. You get constant output, constant feedback, constant momentum. And then you switch to the work that doesn't have that — making a decision with incomplete information, following up with someone who hasn't replied, sitting with an idea you're not sure about."
>
> "And that shift feels brutal. Not because the slow work got harder. Because your nervous system got used to a completely different speed of reward."

### Caption

tl;dr: my unhinged thesis on why I can mass-produce code all day but can't reply to one email

### Production notes

- No overlay text. No cuts. Just you talking.
- The hook ("I could've written this as a text post in 30 seconds") should feel offhand, not rehearsed.
- The close — "I don't think I'm the only one" — is an invitation, not a call to action. Say it like you actually mean it.
- Film wherever you work. Phone propped up. Don't set up a shot.
- Read the script a few times, then put it away and say it in your own words.

### Platform captions

- **LinkedIn:** "I could've typed this in 30 seconds. But that's kind of the problem."
- **X:** No caption, or just "feedback loops."

## Video 2: "The Identity Problem"

**Post:** Thursday or Friday
**Length:** ~22 seconds
**Format:** Talking head. Look at camera. No screen share, no overlay text, no cuts.

### Script

> "I got laid off after 12 years as an engineer. And instead of looking for another job I decided to try building something on my own."
>
> "Not because I had some great startup idea. Because I realized that coding — the thing I'd built my whole identity around — stopped feeling like problem solving and started feeling like project management."
>
> "So now I'm trying to figure out what I'm actually good at when the part I was good at stopped being the hard part."

### Production notes

- This is the scarier one to post. That's how you know it's the right one.
- The last line is the one that lands. Don't rush it. Let yourself sit with it for a beat before you stop recording.
- Don't end with a call to action. Don't ask people to follow. Just stop.
- Same setup as Video 1 — phone, real space, no production.

### Platform captions

- **LinkedIn:** "12 years as an engineer. Then the hard part changed."
- **X:** No caption.

## Future video ideas (unscripted)

These are angles for future weeks, not fully scripted yet. Each one explores a different facet of the psychology.

**"The Engineer's Comfort Zone"**
The gap between solving technical problems (where there's always a next step) and solving business problems (where there's just silence). Engineers don't know what to do with silence.

**"The Irony"**
Having ADHD and building a focus app. Being both the founder and the target user, and the target user is winning.

**"The Skills That Don't Transfer"**
12 years of breaking big problems into small problems. Works great for code, terribly for "figure out if anyone wants this."

**"The First Cold Email"**
Mass-producing code with AI all day. Three sentences to a stranger nearly broke you. The gap between those two experiences.

**"The Quiet Part"**
The loneliness of going from a team of engineers to just you. Not the coding part — the deciding part. Missing having someone to argue with.

## General recording tips

- Film both in one sitting if you can. Same lighting, same energy.
- Phone camera is fine. Vertical (9:16) for both platforms.
- Don't over-rehearse. Read the script a few times, then say it in your own words. The slight imperfection is the point.
- If a take feels too polished, do it again worse.
- Look at the lens, not the screen. Glancing away briefly while thinking is fine — that's what people do in real conversation.
